% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Component-level validation is insufficient for composed adaptive designs---trials
in which Bayesian monitoring, sample size re-estimation (SSR), and
response-adaptive randomization (RAR) update based on overlapping information sets
at interim analyses.  We characterize two failure mechanisms---prior-data conflict
and time trends---and show through simulation that they inflate Type~I error
(T1E) at the pipeline level even when every component individually passes its
validation check.  Under a mild conflict (2\,pp above the prior mean), T1E
reaches 0.0771 ($+$208\% above nominal $\alpha = 0.025$); a negative control
moving SSR to an earlier interim shows negligible attenuation, identifying
prior-data conflict as the dominant driver.  In mechanism-isolation analysis, the two mechanisms are super-additive
(interaction $= +0.0278$, 95\% SI excluding zero).
Component-level calibration guarantees are not invariant under composition; the
failure is invisible without pipeline-level evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The accelerating adoption of AI-assisted clinical trial design software has
created a validation gap.  Statisticians and sponsors now routinely use
computational tools to specify priors, simulate operating characteristics, and
prepare regulatory submissions---yet most tools lack systematic validation against
reference implementations, cannot guarantee reproducibility under stochastic
simulation, and fail to account for the behavior of combined adaptive mechanisms.

The FDA's January 2026 draft guidance on Bayesian adaptive designs
\citep{fda_bayesian_2026} makes this gap concrete.  For the first time, the
guidance explicitly requires sponsors to document operating characteristics under
realistic simulation scenarios, pre-specify how prior-data conflict will be
handled, and demonstrate that Bayesian adaptive designs meet frequentist operating
characteristic targets.  These requirements expose a specific deficiency: most
validation practice operates at the \emph{component level}, evaluating each
adaptive mechanism in isolation, without examining the behavior of the composed
system.

This paper makes two claims.  First, the methodological claim:
\textbf{component-level validation is insufficient for composed adaptive
designs}.  When multiple adaptive mechanisms update based on overlapping
information sets at interim analyses, the operating characteristics of the
composed system cannot be guaranteed by calibrating each component independently.
Second, the empirical finding: in the specific design class studied here,
\textbf{prior-data conflict is the dominant source of Type~I error inflation},
amplified by SSR and RAR but not primarily caused by the timing of any single
shared interim.  We state the first claim formally, demonstrate both through
simulation, and use a negative control to identify the primary driver.

We present Zetyra as the reproducible evaluation infrastructure for this work.
The platform is evidence and infrastructure for the argument; it is not the
argument itself.

\section{Background}

\subsection{The Validation Gap in Clinical Trial Design Software}

Validation of clinical trial design software is inconsistently defined in
practice.  In regulatory submissions, sponsors typically document that design
outputs were computed using a named software package, sometimes with cross-checks
against published values.  This informal approach does not constitute systematic
benchmarking: it does not establish maximum numerical deviation across the full
range of design parameters, does not guarantee reproducibility under stochastic
methods, and does not account for the behavior of multiple adaptive components
operating jointly.  The FDA January 2026 draft guidance raises the standard explicitly:
operating characteristics must be evaluated under realistic scenarios---including
prior-data conflict, misspecification, and time trends---and must be documented
in a reproducible, auditable manner.

\subsection{Composed Adaptive Designs and the Shared Information Problem}

\textbf{Definition.}  A \emph{composed adaptive design} is a design in which two
or more adaptive mechanisms update based on overlapping information sets at one or
more interim analyses.  Formally, let $\Ft$ denote the information set available
at interim time~$t$.  A design is composed in the relevant sense if there exist
adaptive mechanisms $\Di$ and $\Dj$ such that $\Fi \cap \Fj \neq \emptyset$ for
some $t$.

This definition distinguishes designs where interaction effects are mechanistically
expected from designs with staggered or informationally independent updates.  The
standard validation approach calibrates each component independently under its
marginal model.  The implicit assumption---that joint control follows from marginal
control---fails when components share an interim information set.

\subsection{Evaluation Infrastructure}

The simulations in this paper are implemented using Zetyra, a validated
browser-based calculator suite for adaptive and Bayesian clinical trial design.
Each adaptive component is validated against established reference engines
(gsDesign, RBesT, pwr, scipy) and published trial outcomes; the full validation
framework is described in Appendix~\ref{app:platform}.  All stochastic methods
use fixed random seeds, and the complete simulation code is publicly available.

\section{Composed Design Evaluation}

\subsection{Formal Argument: Why Marginal Calibration Fails}

Let $\phi(X)$ denote the final rejection rule (Bayesian monitoring), mapping
observed data $X$ to $\{0, 1\}$, calibrated under the data-generating law
$P_0$ (the null) so that $P_0\!\bigl(\phi(X) = 1\bigr) \leq \alpha$.  Let
$A_1(X_t), A_2(X_t)$ denote adaptation rules---SSR and RAR respectively---that
alter sample size or allocation based on interim data $X_t$ but do not
themselves reject $H_0$.  Each adaptation rule is validated independently
(e.g., SSR preserves conditional power targets; RAR tracks
$P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$ within tolerance).

The composed decision rule is
$\phi_{\text{comp}}(X) = \phi\!\bigl(X;\, A_1(X_t),\, A_2(X_t)\bigr)$: the
final rejection indicator evaluated on data whose sample size and allocation
were shaped by the adaptation rules.  The standard validation approach
calibrates $\phi$ under fixed design parameters and validates each $A_j$
independently.  The implicit assumption---that
$P_0\!\bigl(\phi_{\text{comp}}(X) = 1\bigr) \leq \alpha$ follows from marginal
calibration---holds when the adaptation rules operate on informationally
independent subsets of $X$.  When they share $\Ft$, this guarantee need not
hold:
\[
  P_0\!\bigl(\phi_{\text{comp}}(X) = 1\bigr) \text{ may exceed } \alpha
  \quad \text{when} \quad \Fi \cap \Fj \neq \emptyset.
\]
The formal condition identifies when pipeline-level inflation is
\emph{possible}; the dominant source in practice depends on the design.  In
the class studied here, prior-data conflict is the primary driver, amplified
by but not contingent on the specific interim at which adaptive decisions
co-occur (Section~5.3).  Figure~\ref{fig:composed} illustrates the structure.

  \caption{Composed adaptive design: three components (Bayesian monitoring,
  single-arm SSR, RAR), each individually validated (\textsc{pass}), converge on a

  The composed decision pipeline inherits the dependence structure, and

\end{figure}

\subsection{Mechanism 1: Prior-Data Conflict}

Under mild prior-data conflict---when the current trial data are inconsistent with
the informative historical prior---the posterior probability threshold calibrated
under the null can be crossed more easily than the marginal calibration
anticipates.  The Bayesian monitoring becomes anti-conservative at the pipeline
level.  This mechanism is activated specifically when the prior is informative and
the information set at the interim includes contributions from both current and
historical data.

\subsection{Mechanism 2: Time Trends Under Adaptive Randomization}

Response-adaptive randomization shifts allocation toward better-performing arms
based on accumulating outcome data.  Under moderate time trends---drift in baseline
rates due to changes in standard of care, enrollment patterns, or site
effects---RAR introduces systematic imbalance that biases the final analysis.  This
mechanism is not detectable at the RAR component level, where validation typically
assumes time-stationarity.

\subsection{The Interaction That Drives Type I Error Inflation}

When prior-data conflict and time trends co-occur in a composed pipeline, their
effects reinforce each other at interim looks.  The specific interaction: the SSR
trigger and the Bayesian posterior update both compute from the same interim data
$\mathcal{F}_{45}$.  The SSR rule increases sample size in response to an estimate
that has already been distorted by the time trend; the posterior update is
anti-conservative due to prior-data conflict.  This co-occurrence is invisible to
component-level validation.

\section{Simulation Study}

\subsection{Design Specification}

We consider a class of two-arm Phase~II oncology designs with historical borrowing
on the control arm via a MAP prior, and three co-occurring adaptive mechanisms
sharing interim information sets.

The specific instance used for simulation: a two-arm Phase~II oncology design with
historical borrowing on the control arm via a MAP prior \citep{weber_rbest}, with
three looks at $n = 30, 45, 60$ and a final at $n \geq 90$ (post-SSR).  MAP prior:
initial approximation Beta(6.5, 19.5) with 70\% weight (ESS~$\approx$~26, mean
$\approx$~0.25); the simulation uses the fitted robustified mixture from RBesT
(informative component $w = 0.589$, $a = 18.4$, $b = 53.3$, plus
additional mixture components).  The historical cohorts ($n = 50, 40, 60$;
response rates 26\%, 25\%, 25\%) are illustrative but representative of the
sample sizes and inter-study variation seen in Phase~II oncology control arms.
The fitted prior mean ($\approx 0.25$) defines the ``no conflict'' baseline;
the ``mild'' and ``strong'' conflict scenarios ($p = 0.27, 0.30$) correspond
to 2\,pp and 5\,pp drift above this baseline, spanning the range of plausible inter-study control-rate variation
documented in oncology settings \citep{viele_2014,tang_2010}.
Blinded SSR at 50\% enrollment using pooled response estimation across both
arms (``single-arm'' in the sense that the SSR decision uses only aggregate
counts, not treatment-arm allocation), validated against \citet{lee_liu_2008}.  Thompson Sampling RAR across
two arms, validated against analytical $P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$.  Reference trial:
NCT03377023 \citep{nct03377023}.

Null hypothesis: $p_e = p_c$ (no treatment effect).  Under prior-data conflict,
both arms are generated from a common rate $p > 0.25$ while the MAP prior expects
$p_c = 0.25$---preserving $H_0$ while introducing a conflict between the
historical prior and the current data-generating process.

\textit{Clarification of the estimand.}  The rejection probabilities reported
throughout this paper are Type~I error rates under the data-generating null
($p_e = p_c$), not posterior predictive error rates conditional on prior
beliefs.  The prior-data conflict is a realistic scenario in which the
historical prior is misspecified relative to the current trial population, but
$H_0$ holds in the current data.  The inflated rejection probability is
therefore frequentist Type~I error under the current-trial data-generating
null---not a Bayesian robustness diagnostic.  This distinction matters:
the inflation we report would lead to erroneous regulatory conclusions, not
merely suboptimal Bayesian inference.

We evaluate two conflict scenarios:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Mild conflict} ($p = 0.27$, 2\,pp above prior mean): the
    headline scenario, representative of plausible drift in historical control
    rates.  Seed~2028.
  \item \textbf{Strong conflict} ($p = 0.30$, 5\,pp above prior mean): a stress
    test demonstrating that inflation scales with conflict severity.  Seed~2026.
\end{itemize}

Time trend: linear drift $\delta = 0.05$ over full enrollment in all conditions.
Sensitivity to conflict magnitude, prior weight, trend severity, and SSR timing
is examined in Section~5.6.

\textit{Note on calibration:} The efficacy threshold $\gamma^* = 0.970$ is
selected from a grid $[0.90, 0.99]$ by 0.005 steps, yielding calibration T1E =
0.0290 (target: 0.025).  The calibrated rate exceeds nominal $\alpha$ by
0.004, a consequence of the coarse 0.005-step grid; the next grid point
($\gamma^* = 0.975$) yields T1E below 0.025.  The coarse grid introduces
small calibration error in both directions; a finer binary search is planned
for the final paper, and all headline estimates should be rechecked under
exact calibration.

\subsection{Component-Level Validation}

Each adaptive component is individually validated.  MAP prior: maximum deviation
from RBesT mixture = $1.78 \times 10^{-15}$ (floating-point precision).
SSR minimality check: \texttt{ssr\_n\_target()} finds the minimum $n^*$
satisfying $\mathrm{CP}(n^*) \geq 0.80$; verified that $\mathrm{CP}(n^*) \geq 0.80$
and $\mathrm{CP}(n^*-1) < 0.80$ for all non-futile, non-boundary scenarios.
RAR one-step allocation: maximum deviation from analytical
$P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$ = 0.0036 (Monte Carlo error at $n = 100{,}000$).
All three components pass their validation checks.

\subsection{Composed Pipeline: Primary and Version~B Negative Control}

\noindent\textbf{Version~B negative control design:}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Design & $n=30$ & $n=45$ & $n=60$ & Final \\
\midrule
Primary    & Monitor & Monitor + SSR & Monitor & Monitor \\
Control~B  & Monitor + SSR & Monitor & Monitor & Monitor \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Monitoring is evaluated at identical sample sizes in both designs.  The only
difference is when SSR fires relative to the monitoring looks.  This is the
minimum change needed to remove co-occurrence at $n=45$, where conflict and trend
effects have had more time to accumulate.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Calibration:} $\gamma^* = 0.970$, yielding T1E = 0.0290 under
$H_0$ with no conflict or trend (target: $\alpha = 0.025$; coarse grid artifact
noted in Section~5.6).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Conflict severity comparison} ($n = 10{,}000$ replications each):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Scenario & $p$ (both arms) & T1E & vs.\ nominal \\
\midrule
Calibration (no conflict, no trend) & 0.25 & 0.0290 & --- \\
Mild conflict + trend (headline)    & 0.27 & \textbf{0.0771} & $+$208\% \\
Strong conflict + trend (stress test) & 0.30 & 0.1274 & $+$410\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip\noindent
\textit{Monte Carlo uncertainty:} All T1E estimates carry binomial Monte Carlo
standard errors (MCSE): $\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/N_{\text{sim}}}$.
For $N = 10{,}000$: MCSE $\approx 0.0027$ at T1E $= 0.08$, $\approx 0.0033$
at T1E $= 0.13$.  For $N = 5{,}000$ (factorial, mechanism isolation):
MCSE $\approx 0.0038$--$0.0048$.  Full MCSEs and 95\% simulation intervals
are reported in the simulation supplement.

\smallskip
Under the mild conflict scenario, pipeline-level T1E reaches \textbf{0.0771}
(MCSE = 0.0027), an absolute inflation of $+0.0521$ above the nominal
$\alpha = 0.025$ ($+$208\%).
This is a realistic conflict scenario: a 2\,pp drift in historical control rates
is well within the range of inter-study variation in oncology.  All three
components individually pass their validation checks; the inflation is invisible
to component-level evaluation.

The strong conflict scenario ($p = 0.30$, 5\,pp drift, T1E = 0.1274) serves as
a stress test, demonstrating that inflation scales monotonically with conflict
severity.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Version~B negative control} (SSR at $n=30$, same conflict + trend; seed 2027):

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item T1E = \textbf{0.1266} (strong conflict), attenuation = 0.8\% of primary inflation
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
The results are summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:results}.  The minimal attenuation when SSR is moved from $n=45$ to $n=30$ is itself
informative: the inflation is \emph{not} primarily driven by SSR--monitor
co-occurrence at a specific interim, but rather by the MAP prior conflict
persistently biasing $P(p_e > p_c)$ at \emph{every} monitoring look.  SSR
amplifies the effect through extended sample size (more monitoring events), not
through its timing relative to a specific posterior update.  The primary
driver is persistent MAP prior anti-conservatism under conflict, amplified by
the SSR--RAR composition.

\subsection{Factorial Decomposition: SSR $\times$ RAR}

Four designs (SSR $\times$ RAR, all with conflict + trend) decompose the
inflation:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Effect & Estimate & Direction \\
\midrule
$\Delta_\text{SSR}$ & $+0.0128$ & SSR inflates T1E \\
$\Delta_\text{RAR}$ & $+0.0124$ & RAR inflates T1E \\
$\Delta_\text{interaction}$ & $-0.0102$ & Slightly sub-additive \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Under H$_0$ with prior-data conflict, both SSR and RAR independently inflate
T1E.  SSR contributes by extending sample size ($\Delta_\text{SSR} = +0.0128$),
giving more monitoring events at which the anti-conservative posterior can fire.
RAR contributes by shifting allocation based on biased interim estimates
($\Delta_\text{RAR} = +0.0124$).  The slightly sub-additive interaction
($\Delta_\text{interaction} = -0.0102$; propagated MCSE $\approx 0.009$)
is within Monte Carlo noise and should not be over-interpreted; at this
replication count the sign is not distinguishable from zero.  The result is
consistent with approximately additive contributions from SSR and RAR,
both drawing from the same prior-data conflict signal.

\subsection{Mechanism Isolation: Prior-Data Conflict $\times$ Time Trend}

A 2$\times$2 experiment (conflict $\times$ trend) with primary design settings:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Condition & T1E & vs.\ calibration \\
\midrule
Neither (calibration, seed 4000) & 0.0292 & --- \\
Conflict only & 0.0850 & $+0.0558$ \\
Trend only & 0.0514 & $+0.0222$ \\
Both (primary) & 0.1350 & $+0.1058$ \\
\midrule
Additive prediction & 0.1072 & \\
Interaction term & $+0.0278$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Both mechanisms independently inflate T1E in the correct direction.
Prior-data conflict alone produces substantial inflation ($+0.0558$): the MAP
prior persistently underestimates the control arm, biasing the posterior
comparison at every monitoring look.  Time trends alone contribute $+0.0222$
through RAR allocation imbalance.  Crucially, the interaction term is
\emph{positive} ($+0.0278$; propagated MCSE $\approx 0.009$, 95\% SI
$[+0.010, +0.046]$, excluding zero): the observed T1E under both mechanisms
(0.1350) exceeds the additive prediction (0.1072), demonstrating
\textbf{super-additivity} (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).
The two mechanisms amplify each other beyond the sum of their individual
contributions.  This is the key finding: in composed adaptive designs under
realistic perturbations, the inflation is not merely additive---it is driven by
the interaction between miscalibrated priors and adaptive allocation under time
trends.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

The mild conflict scenario ($p = 0.27$, T1E = 0.0771) is the headline result;
the following sweeps use the strong conflict parameterisation ($p = 0.30$) to
characterize mechanism sensitivity across a wider range.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Conflict severity} ($p \in \{0.25, 0.27, 0.30\}$): T1E increases
    monotonically from 0.0290 (no conflict) to 0.0771 ($+2$\,pp) to 0.1274
    ($+5$\,pp), with no direction reversal.
  \item \textbf{Prior weight} (0--90\%): inflation monotone increasing; T1E
    ranges 0.1144--0.1338 under strong conflict, no reversal.
  \item \textbf{Time trend} $\delta$ (0--15\%): inflation monotone increasing;
    T1E ranges 0.0858--0.2166, no reversal.
  \item \textbf{SSR trigger timing} ($n_\text{SSR} \in \{20, 30, 40, 45, 55, 60\}$):
    T1E ranges 0.1206--0.1346 under corrected H0 ($p_e = p_c = p_{\text{conflict}}$);
    inflation persists across all timing values with no direction reversal,
    consistent with prior-data conflict rather than SSR timing as the primary driver.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Calibration note:} $\gamma^* = 0.970$ is selected
from a grid with step 0.005, yielding calibration T1E = 0.0290 rather than the
nominal 0.025 target.  The calibrated rate is slightly anti-conservative due to
the coarse grid (the next grid point at 0.975 undershoots); a finer binary
search is planned for the final paper.  Headline estimates should be
rechecked under exact calibration.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Regulatory Implications}

\textbf{Component-level calibration guarantees are not invariant under composition
when adaptive mechanisms share interim information sets.}  Therefore, regulatory
evaluation of operating characteristics must be performed at the level of the full
decision pipeline.

The FDA January 2026 draft guidance requires simulation-based operating characteristics under
realistic scenarios.  The present finding specifies what ``realistic'' must
include: the full composed pipeline, under prior-data conflict and time trends,
not a sequence of isolated component evaluations.

\subsection{Connection to FDA 2026 Bayesian Draft Guidance}

The guidance requires pre-specification of how prior-data conflict will be handled
and documentation of operating characteristics across realistic simulation
scenarios.  The prior-data conflict mechanism characterized in Section~4.2
directly corresponds to the conflict-sensitivity evaluation the guidance calls for.
The simulation framework provides a reproducible template for meeting this
requirement at the pipeline level.

\subsection{What Sponsors Should Do Differently}

Two practical changes, neither requiring new methodology: (1)~evaluate operating
characteristics at the pipeline level, simulating the full composed decision
pathway rather than component-wise; (2)~include prior-data conflict and time trend
scenarios as standard sensitivity conditions.

\subsection{Mitigation Strategies}

The failure mode identified here admits several practical safeguards, none
requiring new methodology.  First, \emph{robust mixture priors}: increasing the
vague component weight reduces prior influence and narrows the gap between the
historical prior and the current data, directly attenuating the conflict
mechanism.  Second, \emph{dynamic borrowing} or \emph{commensurate priors}:
methods that automatically down-weight the historical prior when conflict is
detected can reduce anti-conservatism at the pipeline level, though they must
themselves be validated under composition.  Third, \emph{conflict diagnostics}:
pre-specified checks (e.g., tail-area probabilities comparing the interim
likelihood to the prior predictive) can trigger protocol-defined fallback
thresholds when conflict exceeds a pre-specified bound.  Fourth,
\emph{pipeline-level recalibration}: re-tuning the efficacy threshold
$\gamma^*$ under conflict and trend scenarios, rather than under the
no-conflict null alone, directly addresses the inflation at its source.
Finally, \emph{pre-specified sensitivity thresholds}: regulatory submissions
can include a pre-defined maximum T1E under conflict scenarios (e.g., T1E
$\leq 0.05$ under 2\,pp conflict), making the design's robustness envelope
explicit and auditable.

\emph{Caveat:} Each mitigation strategy itself alters the composed pipeline's
operating characteristics and must therefore be evaluated at the pipeline level,
not assumed to restore nominal control based on component-level reasoning alone.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

This paper establishes the failure mode through simulation in a specific class of
designs.  A closed-form characterization of inflation magnitude as a function of
design parameters is ongoing work.  The mechanism isolation simulations preserve
H0 ($p_e = p_c = p_{\text{conflict}}$) throughout; extension to alternative
null specifications (e.g., varying conflict magnitude under H0) is future work.  A
forthcoming collaboration on the Analysis of Credibility (AnCred) framework
\citep{matthews_forthcoming} will provide a principled approach to prior
specification and conflict detection directly relevant to Section~4.2.

\section{Conclusion}

Component-level validation is not enough for composed adaptive designs.  The
operating characteristics of the system are not the sum of its parts.  When
adaptive mechanisms update on overlapping information sets, per-component
calibration guarantees do not transfer to the composed pipeline---and the failure
is not visible without pipeline-level evaluation.

Zetyra provides a validated, reproducible framework for evaluating composed
designs as composed systems.  Under a mild prior-data conflict representative of
plausible inter-study rate variation, pipeline-level T1E reached 0.0771 ($+$208\%
above nominal)---with the two mechanisms super-additive, producing 0.0278 more
inflation than the sum of their individual contributions.  The simulation code and validation scripts are publicly available for
independent verification and extension; the Zetyra platform is a commercial
SaaS product.

\medskip\noindent\textit{Validate the pipeline.  Not the parts.}

\section{Platform Validation and Infrastructure}
\label{app:platform}

Zetyra spans four design domains: frequentist methods (group sequential design,
survival sample size, CUPED); Bayesian methods (prior elicitation, historical
borrowing via MAP and power priors, sequential monitoring, predictive power);
adaptive methods (blinded, unblinded, and single-arm sample size re-estimation
(SSR); response-adaptive randomization (RAR) via Thompson Sampling and doubly
adaptive biased coin designs); and master protocol settings (basket, umbrella,
platform trials).

\paragraph{Reference engines.}
Frequentist validation uses gsDesign \citep{anderson_gsdesign} for group
sequential boundaries and Schoenfeld for survival power.  SSR rules are validated
against conditional power formulas via self-consistency testing.  Bayesian
validation uses RBesT \citep{weber_rbest} for MAP prior construction, and
\citet{zhou_lee_yuan_2017} for sequential monitoring boundaries.
Single-arm SSR is validated against \citet{lee_liu_2008} and
\citet{saville_2014}.  RAR designs are validated against analytical
$P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$ computed via numerical integration.

\paragraph{Clinical trial benchmarks.}
Nine published trials provide outcome-level validation: I-SPY~2, STAMPEDE,
REMAP-CAP, NCT03377023 (Nivo+Ipi+Nintedanib NSCLC), GSD PACIFIC~OS,
GSD MONALEESA-7~OS, and Bayesian sequential survival benchmarks
\citep{barker_ispy2,angus_remapcap}.  Maximum deviation: 0.034 z-score units.

\paragraph{Automated testing.}
An automated suite of 655 tests across 35 scripts runs continuously on GitHub
Actions and is open to independent verification:
\url{github.com/evidenceinthewild/zetyra-validation}.
Full documentation is available in the Zetyra Technical White Paper
\citep{zetyra_whitepaper}.
