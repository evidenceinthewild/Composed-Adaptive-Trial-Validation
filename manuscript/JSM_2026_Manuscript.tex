% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Efficacy thresholds for Bayesian monitoring are commonly calibrated at a single
assumed control rate.  We show by simulation that in a two-arm Phase~II design
with historical borrowing on the control arm, such point calibration does not
deliver Type~I error (T1E) control across the composite null.  With the
threshold calibrated to $\alpha = 0.025$ at the design anchor $p_0 = 0.25$
with no time trend, we evaluate a one-dimensional slice of the null in which a
shared time trend is active throughout.  Along that slice T1E rises
monotonically with the common baseline rate, reaching 0.2618 at $p = 0.36$ with
all adaptive mechanisms active.  Ablations that disable individual components while
\emph{holding the full-design threshold fixed} show that the excess is not
created by the adaptive components alone: with SSR and RAR both disabled, the
monitoring rule under that threshold still yields 0.1170 at $p = 0.30$.
Because those ablations are not separately calibrated, we report them as
ablations rather than as a decomposition of attributable shares.  Sample size
re-estimation (SSR) and response-adaptive randomization (RAR) modify the
excess substantially and nonlinearly---RAR has
essentially no marginal effect without SSR ($-0.0018$) but adds $+0.0314$ when
SSR is active (interaction $+0.0332$, 95\% SI $[+0.0144, +0.0520]$).  Across the
evaluated null, the fixed-$\gamma^*$ difference between the full design and the
SSR-and-RAR-disabled ablation \emph{reverses sign}, from $-0.0212$ at
$p = 0.20$ to $+0.0886$ at $p = 0.36$.  Departure from the
calibration anchor and time trends are themselves super-additive (interaction
$+0.0288$, 95\% SI $[+0.0136, +0.0440]$).  The grid demonstrates a control
failure but does not locate the supremum.  Operating characteristics should be
evaluated across the composite null rather than at a single assumed rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Computational tools are used to specify priors, simulate operating
characteristics and prepare regulatory submissions.  In the workflow examined
here, the checks applied to such a tool are component-level and numerical: each
adaptive mechanism is compared against a reference implementation in isolation.
This paper asks what those checks do and do not establish about the error rate
of the assembled design.  We make no claim about how widespread that workflow
is; the question is what follows from it where it is used.

The FDA's January 2026 draft guidance on the use of Bayesian methodology in
clinical trials \citep{fda_bayesian_2026} is directly relevant.  The guidance recommends that
sponsors document operating characteristics under realistic simulation
scenarios, pre-specify how prior-data conflict will be handled, and evaluate
whether Bayesian adaptive designs meet frequentist operating characteristic
targets.  (It is a draft guidance containing nonbinding recommendations, and we
describe it as such throughout.)  These recommendations bear on a specific
practice, and one this paper examines directly: an efficacy threshold
calibrated at a single assumed control rate, with that single point then
treated as evidence of error control.

This paper makes two claims, at two levels.  The primary claim is about
\textbf{robustness}: an efficacy threshold calibrated at one assumed control
rate does not deliver Type~I error control across the composite null.  The secondary claim is about \textbf{composition}: SSR and RAR modify
that excess substantially and nonlinearly, and the direction of their combined
effect depends on where in the null the design is evaluated.

Both claims are demonstrated by simulation over a grid of common baseline
rates.  We are explicit about what such a grid can and cannot establish: it
exhibits points at which control fails, but it does not locate or estimate the
supremum of the Type~I error over the null.

We present Zetyra as the reproducible evaluation infrastructure for this work.
The platform is evidence and infrastructure for the argument; it is not the
argument itself.

\section{Background}

\subsection{What Component-Level Checks Establish}

``Validation'' of clinical trial design software is not a term with a single
agreed meaning.  Where it amounts to documenting that design outputs were
computed with a named package, with cross-checks against published values, it
does not establish maximum numerical deviation across the range of design
parameters, does not guarantee reproducibility under stochastic methods, and
does not account for the behaviour of multiple adaptive components operating
jointly.  The FDA January 2026 draft guidance raises the standard explicitly:
operating characteristics should be evaluated by comprehensive simulation over
plausible assumptions---the guidance discusses prior-data conflict explicitly
and calls for scenarios spanning plausible values of design inputs, including
the background rate of the endpoint---and documented reproducibly.

The simulations here are implemented using Zetyra, a validated browser-based
calculator suite for adaptive and Bayesian trial design.  Each adaptive
component is benchmarked against established reference engines (gsDesign,
RBesT, pwr, scipy) and published trial outcomes; the framework is described in
Appendix~\ref{app:platform}.  All stochastic methods use fixed random seeds,
and the simulation source that produced every number reported here is released
with the paper (see Acknowledgements).  Fixed seeds alone do not confer exact
reproducibility without matching package versions, which are recorded in the
session information of the simulation document.

\subsection{Composed Adaptive Designs and Marginal Calibration}

\textbf{Definition.}  A \emph{composed adaptive design} is a design in which two
or more adaptive mechanisms read the same interim data before the final
analysis.  In the design studied here, Bayesian monitoring, the SSR trigger and
the RAR allocation rule all compute from the accumulated responses at interim
looks.

We do not formalise this as a set-theoretic condition on information sets.
Writing $\Fi \cap \Fj \neq \emptyset$ would not distinguish anything: for
sigma-fields the intersection always contains $\emptyset$ and $\Omega$, so the
condition holds trivially for any pair.  Nor is the converse available---
informational independence does not by itself guarantee that error control
survives adaptation.

The relevant observation is weaker and purely negative.  Let $\phi(X)$ denote
the final rejection rule (Bayesian monitoring), and let $A_1(X_t)$, $A_2(X_t)$
denote the adaptation rules---SSR and RAR---which alter sample size or
allocation based on interim data $X_t$ but do not themselves reject $H_0$.
Each is validated independently (SSR preserves conditional-power targets; RAR
tracks $P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$ within tolerance).  The composed decision rule
is $\phi_{\text{comp}}(X) = \phi\!\bigl(X;\, A_1(X_t),\, A_2(X_t)\bigr)$: the
final rejection indicator evaluated on data whose sample size and allocation
were shaped by the adaptation rules.  The standard approach calibrates that
composed rule at a single point, choosing $\gamma^*$ so that
$P_{p_0,\,\delta=0}\!\bigl(\phi_{\text{comp}}(X) = 1\bigr) \approx \alpha$ at
one anchor rate with no trend.  That establishes the error rate at that point
and nowhere else: it places no bound on
$P_{p,\,\delta}\!\bigl(\phi_{\text{comp}}(X) = 1\bigr)$ for other $(p, \delta)$
in the null.  The component benchmark checks add no such bound either, since
none of them evaluates an error rate.

\textbf{Calibrating each component under its own marginal model, and the
composed rule at a single null point, places no constraint on the error rate of
the composed rule elsewhere in the null.}  This is a statement about what those
checks fail to establish, not a condition under which inflation must occur.
Whether it occurs, in which direction and by how much are empirical questions,
which is why the remainder of the paper is a simulation study.
Figure~\ref{fig:composed} illustrates the structure.



\textit{How this grid should be read.}  Three limits govern it, and they apply
equally to every result that follows.  We do not claim a crossing point: the
effects at $p = 0.25$ ($-0.0070$) and $p = 0.27$ ($+0.0078$) are each within
Monte Carlo error, so the sweep establishes the direction change, not its
location.  The SSR rule is a pooled one-sample conditional-power test against
$p_0 = 0.25$ while the estimand is the two-arm comparison, so because SSR
activates near that same value the apparent reversal location may partly
reflect the rule's construction.  And uniform Type~I error control over the
composite null requires T1E $\leq \alpha$ at \emph{every} point in it: the grid contains multiple
clear violations and therefore \emph{demonstrates} failure, but it does not
locate the supremum, establishing only that it is at least 0.2618 --- the
largest value observed, at the upper grid boundary, where the curve was still
rising.

\section{Simulation Study}

\subsection{Design Specification}
\label{sec:design}

We consider a class of two-arm Phase~II oncology designs with historical borrowing
on the control arm via a MAP prior, and three co-occurring adaptive mechanisms
sharing interim information sets.

The specific instance used for simulation: a two-arm Phase~II oncology design with
historical borrowing on the control arm via a MAP prior \citep{weber_rbest}, with
three looks at $n = 30, 45, 60$ and a final at $n \geq 90$ (post-SSR).  The MAP
prior is fitted with RBesT from the historical cohorts ($n = 50, 40, 60$;
response rates 26\%, 25\%, 25\%; pooled rate $38/150 = 0.2533$) and
robustified, giving a three-component mixture: $w = 0.5887$,
Beta(18.403, 53.273); $w = 0.2113$, Beta(1.956, 4.703); and a flat
$w = 0.2000$, Beta(1, 1).
Blinded SSR at 50\% enrollment using pooled response estimation across both
arms (``single-arm'' in the sense that the SSR decision uses only aggregate
counts, not treatment-arm allocation).  The SSR calculation is checked against
conditional-power and sample-size formulas by self-consistency, not against an
external reference design.  Thompson Sampling RAR across two arms, benchmarked
against analytical $P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$.  The separate
predictive-probability monitoring component of the platform is benchmarked
against \citet{lee_liu_2008} and \citet{saville_2014}, and additionally
against the design and decisions reported for NCT03377023
\citep{nct03377023}; that single-arm trial is not the reference design for the
two-arm composed pipeline studied here.

\textit{The reference rate is ambiguous, and we name it explicitly.}  The
threshold is calibrated at the design anchor $p_0 = 0.25$.  That anchor is not
the mean of any prior in play: the dominant informative component has mean
0.2568, the full robustified mixture has mean 0.3132, and the historical
pooled rate is 0.2533.  Both scenarios evaluated below therefore lie
\emph{below} the mixture mean.  Throughout we describe $p > 0.25$ as
\textbf{departure of the baseline rate from the calibration anchor}, never as
``$k$ percentage points above the prior mean''---a description that is false
against every candidate reference.

Null hypothesis: $p_e = p_c$ (no treatment effect), evaluated at a common
baseline rate $p$ with the shared time trend active.  Both arms are generated
from the same baseline and drift together, so $H_0$ holds throughout while the
realised rate departs from the anchor.

\textit{Clarification of the estimand.}  The rejection probabilities reported
throughout are Type~I error rates under the data-generating null
($p_e = p_c$), not posterior predictive error rates conditional on prior
beliefs.  The inflated rejection probability is frequentist Type~I error under
the current-trial data-generating null---not a Bayesian robustness diagnostic.

We evaluate two departure scenarios, each with the trend active:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Mild departure} ($p = 0.27$, $+2$\,pp from the anchor).  Seed~2028.
  \item \textbf{Strong departure} ($p = 0.30$, $+5$\,pp from the anchor): a
    stress test.  Seed~2026.
\end{itemize}

On the scale of these departures, \citet{tang_2010} treat a
five-percentage-point control-rate drift as clinically modest in an oncology
simulation and report Type~I error two to four times the projected level under
such drift; the two-point scenario is included here as a smaller sensitivity
departure.  \citet{viele_2014} review historical-borrowing methods and the
conditions under which current and historical control rates diverge.  Neither
establishes an empirical 2--5 percentage-point range, and we do not claim
one.
Time trend: linear drift $\delta = 0.05$ over the planned enrollment horizon in
all conditions.

\textit{Monte Carlo uncertainty:} All T1E estimates carry binomial Monte Carlo
standard errors (MCSE): $\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/N_{\text{sim}}}$.
For $N = 10{,}000$: MCSE $\approx 0.0027$ at T1E $= 0.08$, $\approx 0.0033$ at
T1E $= 0.13$.  For $N = 5{,}000$: factorial cells span MCSE 0.0045--0.0053;
departure~$\times$~trend cells span 0.0022--0.0052.  Full MCSEs and 95\%
simulation intervals are reported in the simulation supplement.  Sensitivity to departure magnitude, vague-component weight,
trend severity, and SSR timing is examined in Section~\ref{sec:sens}.

\textit{Note on calibration:} The efficacy threshold is obtained by grid-free
binary search rather than from a coarse grid, giving $\gamma^* = 0.96596$ with
in-sample T1E $= 0.0250$ (MCSE 0.0011, $N = 20{,}000$) at the design anchor
$p_0 = 0.25$ with no trend.  This is \emph{not} exact calibration: binary
search targets the empirical sample exactly, not the population Type~I error,
and an independent calibration sample would be required to verify
out-of-sample behaviour.  It does remove the residual error left by the earlier
0.005-step grid, which had placed calibration at 0.0290 and so charged that
discrepancy to the scenarios when inflation was quoted against 0.025.


\subsection{Component-Level Benchmark Checks}

Each adaptive component is individually benchmarked.  MAP prior: maximum deviation
from RBesT mixture = $1.78 \times 10^{-15}$ (floating-point precision).
SSR minimality check: \texttt{ssr\_n\_target()} finds the minimum $n^*$
satisfying $\mathrm{CP}(n^*) \geq 0.80$; verified that $\mathrm{CP}(n^*) \geq 0.80$
and $\mathrm{CP}(n^*-1) < 0.80$ for all non-futile, non-boundary scenarios.
RAR one-step allocation: maximum deviation from analytical
$P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$ = 0.0036 (Monte Carlo error at $n = 100{,}000$).
All three components pass their benchmark checks; none of those checks
evaluates Type~I error away from the anchor.

\subsection{Type I Error Across the Composite Null}
\label{sec:composite}

We begin with the sweep, because it is the result the rest of the section
modifies.  The threshold was calibrated at a single point --- $p_0 = 0.25$
with $\delta = 0$, no trend --- but $H_0$ here is composite: it holds whenever
the two arms share a common response process, not only at that point.  We index
the null by the common \emph{baseline} rate $p$; both arms start at $p$ and
drift together under the shared time trend, so the response probability is not
constant within a trial.  Error control is a statement about the supremum over
that set, so a single-point evaluation cannot establish it.

What follows is \textbf{a one-dimensional slice of the composite null}: every
row fixes $\delta = 0.05$ and varies only $p$.  \emph{No row in the table is
the calibration scenario.}  The $p = 0.25$ row shares the anchor rate but not
the trend, so it is a different point in the null from the one at which
$\gamma^*$ was tuned; its value should not be read against the calibration
figure 0.0250 as though the trend were held fixed.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
$p$ & SSR + RAR disabled & All mechanisms & Fixed-$\gamma^*$ difference \\
\midrule
0.20 & 0.0336 & 0.0124 & $-0.0212$ \\
0.22 & 0.0494 & 0.0244 & $-0.0250$ \\
0.25 & 0.0680 & 0.0610 & $-0.0070$ \\
0.27 & 0.0874 & 0.0952 & $+0.0078$ \\
0.30 & 0.1160 & 0.1582 & $+0.0422$ \\
0.33 & 0.1488 & 0.2208 & $+0.0720$ \\
0.36 & 0.1732 & 0.2618 & $+0.0886$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Two readings follow.  First, \textbf{at the full-design threshold, disabling
SSR and RAR does not restore control}: with both switched off, T1E still rises
from 0.0336 at $p = 0.20$ to 0.1732 at $p = 0.36$.  That column is the same fixed-threshold ablation used
throughout (Section~\ref{sec:factorial}) --- it carries $\gamma^*$, which was calibrated
with SSR and RAR active, so it sits near 0.05 at the anchor rather than at
0.025.  Read it as ``the design with the adaptive components switched off,
threshold unchanged'', not as a calibrated monitoring-only design.  Second,
\textbf{at fixed $\gamma^*$, enabling SSR and RAR lowers T1E at low $p$ and
raises it at high $p$}: the difference runs from $-0.0212$ at $p = 0.20$ to
$+0.0886$ at $p = 0.36$, reversing sign across the range.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/composite-null-plot-1.png}
  \caption{Type~I error across the composite null, indexed by the common
  baseline rate $p$.  Both arms start at $p$ and drift together under a shared
  time trend ($\delta = 0.05$), so the response probability rises with
  enrollment and is not constant within a trial.  The grey series holds the full-design threshold with SSR and RAR disabled; it exceeds nominal across
  essentially the whole range.  Adding SSR and RAR (dark green) lowers Type~I

\subsection{Fixed-Threshold Factorial Ablation: SSR $\times$ RAR}
\label{sec:factorial}

Four designs (SSR $\times$ RAR), all at $p = 0.30$ with the trend active:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Condition & T1E & MCSE \\
\midrule
Bayesian monitoring only & 0.1170 & 0.0045 \\
$+$ SSR only             & 0.1348 & 0.0048 \\
$+$ RAR only             & 0.1152 & 0.0045 \\
$+$ SSR and RAR          & 0.1662 & 0.0053 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
We report the four cell values, not only the contrasts.

\textit{These are ablations at a fixed threshold, not a decomposition.}
$\gamma^*$ was calibrated with SSR and RAR \emph{active}
(Section~\ref{sec:design}).  The three ablation cells disable one or both
components while retaining that threshold, so they are not themselves
calibrated to $\alpha$: with SSR and RAR disabled, the monitoring rule under
$\gamma^*$ returns approximately 0.05 at the anchor with no trend, twice
nominal, before any departure or trend is introduced.  Part of the 0.1170
observed at $p = 0.30$ is therefore attributable to applying a threshold tuned
for a different design.  We accordingly do not report attributable shares, and
the reader should not read the baseline cell as ``the excess caused by
monitoring''.  What the row does establish is weaker and still relevant:
disabling both adaptive components does not restore control.

The contrasts are $\Delta_\text{SSR} = +0.0178$, $\Delta_\text{RAR} = -0.0018$,
and $\Delta_\text{interaction} = +0.0332$ (propagated MCSE 0.0096, 95\% SI
$[+0.0144, +0.0520]$).  The interaction is positive and its interval excludes
zero; it was repeated with a fresh seed in the same R implementation (73028,
$N = 3{,}000$ per cell), giving $+0.0343$, 95\% SI $[+0.0101, +0.0585]$.  An
independent Python reimplementation gave a smaller estimate, $+0.0098$, with an
interval containing zero; the magnitude therefore rests on the R runs.

Factorial interaction is symmetric, so we state it in the form that is easiest
to interpret: \textbf{RAR has essentially no marginal effect without SSR
($-0.0018$) but increases T1E by $+0.0314$ when SSR is present}; equivalently,
the SSR effect grows from $+0.0178$ to $+0.0510$ under RAR.  A plausible
mechanism is that SSR extends enrollment, giving RAR more time to accumulate
allocation imbalance, but the present design does not isolate this.

\subsection{Baseline-Rate Departure $\times$ Time Trend}
\label{sec:factors}

A 2$\times$2 experiment (departure $\times$ trend) with primary design settings.
We label this a factor-crossing experiment rather than mechanism isolation:
both factors feed the monitoring rule, the SSR trigger and the RAR allocation
rule simultaneously, and the design does not separate those channels.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Condition & T1E & vs.\ calibration \\
\midrule
Neither (calibration) & 0.0240 & --- \\
Departure only & 0.0894 & $+0.0654$ \\
Trend only & 0.0650 & $+0.0410$ \\
Both (primary) & 0.1592 & $+0.1352$ \\
\midrule
Additive prediction & 0.1304 & \\
Interaction term & $+0.0288$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
Both factors independently raise T1E.  Departure from the anchor alone
produces the larger effect ($+0.0654$): the control prior sits away from the
realised rate, biasing the posterior comparison at every monitoring look.
The trend alone contributes $+0.0410$.  Both cells here use the full design, so
unlike the ablations of Section~\ref{sec:factorial} they share a common
calibrated baseline and the contrasts are internally consistent.

\textit{There is a direct trend channel that does not involve RAR, but it is
smaller than a naive comparison suggests.}  With SSR and RAR disabled and the
full-design threshold retained, T1E at the anchor rises from approximately
0.05 without the trend to 0.0680 with it---an increase of roughly $+0.012$.
The correct contrast is against that ablation's own no-trend value, not against
the nominal 0.025, because the ablation is not calibrated at nominal
(Section~\ref{sec:factorial}).  So a direct channel exists---the trend moves the
realised rate away from the anchor over the enrollment period, which affects
the monitoring rule---but it does not account for the whole trend effect in the
full pipeline.  Departure and trend feed the SSR trigger and the RAR allocation
rule as well.  Isolating an RAR-specific channel would require a
trend~$\times$~RAR factorial, which we did not run.

The interaction term is \emph{positive} ($+0.0288$; propagated MCSE 0.0077, 95\% SI
$[+0.0136, +0.0440]$, excluding zero): the observed T1E under both mechanisms
(0.1592) exceeds the additive prediction (0.1304), demonstrating
\textbf{super-additivity} (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).

Two caveats attach to this interaction.  It is a risk-difference interaction at
the specific factor levels evaluated, not a scale-free mechanistic claim.  And
because drift is anchored to the planned horizon $n_{\text{base}} = 90$, a
trial expanded to $n = 150$ accrues total drift of
$0.05 \times 149/89 \approx 0.084$ rather than 0.05; the interaction therefore
includes the additional secular-trend exposure created by extending enrollment.
That is coherent---a longer trial spans more calendar time---but it should be
reported as such, and a fixed-terminal-drift specification would separate the
two channels.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analyses}
\label{sec:sens}

The following sweeps use the strong departure parameterisation ($p = 0.30$) to
characterize mechanism sensitivity across a wider range.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Departure severity}, at constant trend
    ($p \in \{0.25, 0.27, 0.30\}$, all with $\delta = 0.05$): T1E increases
    monotonically, 0.0610 $\to$ 0.0952 $\to$ 0.1582, with no direction
    reversal.
  \item \textbf{Vague-component weight} (0--90\%): larger weight on the flat
    robustify component means less borrowing; T1E declines monotonically, with
    no reversal.
  \item \textbf{Time trend} $\delta$ (0--15\%): T1E monotone increasing, no reversal.
  \item \textbf{SSR timing, Version~B} (SSR moved from $n = 45$ to $n = 30$,
    strong departure with trend; seed 2027; $N = 10{,}000$): T1E $= 0.1493$
    against 0.1580 for the primary design.  We report the observed difference
    only.  Moving SSR also changes the data on which re-estimation is computed,
    and therefore the realised final-$N$ distribution, so timing is confounded
    with its consequences for final $N$ and the difference does not isolate a
    timing effect.
  \item \textbf{SSR trigger timing} ($n_\text{SSR} \in \{20, 30, 40, 45, 55, 60\}$):
    T1E varies only modestly across timing values with no direction reversal,
    indicating limited sensitivity to trigger timing within the evaluated
    range.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Regulatory Implications}

\textbf{An efficacy threshold calibrated at a single assumed control rate need
not control Type~I error elsewhere in the composite null.}  Evaluation of
operating characteristics should therefore span a range of plausible control
rates rather than a single anchor.

The FDA January 2026 draft guidance recommends simulation-based operating
characteristics under realistic scenarios.  The present finding suggests what
``realistic'' should include for this design class: a grid over the composite
null, with departure from the calibration anchor and time trends crossed rather
than evaluated separately.  The guidance also recommends pre-specifying how
prior-data conflict will be handled; the simulation framework released with
this paper provides a reproducible template for carrying that evaluation out
over the composite null.


\subsection{What Sponsors Should Do Differently}

Three practical changes, none requiring new methodology: (1)~evaluate operating
characteristics over a grid of plausible control rates, not at a single assumed
anchor; (2)~report component ablations alongside the composed design, stating whether
each ablation is separately calibrated --- an ablation that inherits the full
design's threshold shows whether disabling a component restores control, not
how much of the excess that component caused; (3)~cross departure and time-trend scenarios rather
than evaluating them separately, since their interaction is positive.

\subsection{Mitigation Strategies}

The failure mode identified here admits several practical safeguards, none
requiring new methodology.  First, \emph{robust mixture priors}: increasing the
vague component weight reduces prior influence and narrows the gap between the
historical prior and the current data, directly attenuating the
mechanism.  Second, \emph{dynamic borrowing} or \emph{commensurate priors}:
methods that automatically down-weight the historical prior when disagreement is
detected can reduce anti-conservatism at the pipeline level, though they must
themselves be validated under composition.  Third, \emph{disagreement diagnostics}:
pre-specified checks comparing the interim likelihood to the prior predictive
can trigger protocol-defined fallback thresholds when disagreement exceeds a
pre-specified bound.  \citet{qian_matthews_fms_ops} set out such a check in a
form a sponsor can commit to in the protocol and statistical analysis plan,
together with the reporting artefact needed to audit it.  We cite that work as
a template for auditable pre-specification, not as an established remedy for
the present design: its calibration is for Normal-mean settings, and a binary
MAP-mixture design would need its own statistic and its own calibration.  Fourth,
\emph{pipeline-level recalibration}: re-tuning the efficacy threshold
$\gamma^*$ over a grid of baseline rates with the trend active, rather than at the
anchor alone, directly addresses the inflation at its source.
Finally, \emph{pre-specified sensitivity thresholds}: regulatory submissions
can include a pre-defined maximum T1E over a stated range of baseline rates
(e.g., T1E $\leq 0.05$ for departures up to $+2$\,pp from the anchor), making the design's robustness envelope
explicit and auditable.

\emph{Caveat:} Each mitigation strategy itself alters the composed pipeline's
operating characteristics and must therefore be evaluated at the pipeline level,
not assumed to restore nominal control based on component-level reasoning alone.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

This paper demonstrates a control failure by simulation in a specific design
class; several limitations bound what it establishes.

\emph{The supremum is not estimated.}  The grid exhibits points at which
control fails and establishes that the supremum is at least 0.2618, the largest
value observed.  It does not locate the worst case.

\emph{Conflict is not quantified.}  We evaluate departures from the calibration
anchor, not prior-data conflict defined against the prior predictive
distribution.  A prior-predictive diagnostic would place mild and strong on a
principled scale, and would also supply the reference point that
Section~\ref{sec:design} shows to be ambiguous.  Companion work on the
fair-minded scepticism (FMS) prior is relevant but does not directly supply
it: \citet{matthews_qian_fms} derives a null-centred prior for settings where
relevant prior insight is contentious or absent, and
\citet{qian_matthews_fms_ops} set out a pre-specifiable prior--data
disagreement diagnostic in a form a sponsor can commit to.  That framework is
calibrated for \emph{Normal-mean} designs and records explicitly that binary
endpoints do not admit its closed-form identity; the present design satisfies
neither its likelihood nor its sample-size conditions, so adapting it here
would require a new statistic and its own calibration.  That extension has not
been undertaken here.

\emph{The SSR rule is not aligned with the estimand.}  SSR is a pooled
one-sample conditional-power rule against $p_0 = 0.25$, while the final
analysis is a two-arm comparison.  Under $H_0$ with both arms above 0.25,
pooled response exceeds the threshold by construction, so SSR fires on nuisance
drift rather than on treatment effect.  Results involving SSR are conditional
on this choice.

\emph{No sufficient condition is proved.}  For sigma-fields
$\mathcal{F}_i \cap \mathcal{F}_j$ is never empty, and shared information
neither guarantees nor precludes error control on its own; a conditional-error
or design-specific argument would be required.

\emph{Calibration is in-sample.}  Binary search targets the empirical
calibration sample exactly; out-of-sample behaviour is unverified.

\emph{Component ablations are not separately calibrated.}  $\gamma^*$ is
calibrated once, with all mechanisms active.  Every ablation reported here
retains that threshold, so none of them is at nominal even at the anchor: with
SSR and RAR disabled the ablation sits near 0.05 there.  These rows therefore
answer ``does switching this component off restore control?'' and not ``how
much of the excess did this component cause?''.  Separately calibrating each
component design, and reporting differences relative to each design's own
anchor, would support attribution; that analysis is not undertaken here.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

Point calibration did not provide uniform Type~I error control across the
composite null.  At the full-design threshold, disabling SSR and RAR did not
restore control.  Their effects were nonlinear: RAR had essentially no marginal
effect without SSR but raised Type~I error when SSR was active, and the
fixed-$\gamma^*$ contrast reversed sign across the evaluated null rates.

The practical consequence is a reporting standard rather than a new method.
Operating characteristics should be evaluated over a prespecified grid of
null-generating conditions.  Fixed-threshold ablations can show whether
disabling a component restores control, but attribution requires separately
calibrating each design.  The simulation code is publicly available for
independent verification and extension; the Zetyra platform is a commercial
SaaS product.

\medskip\noindent\textit{Calibrate at a point.  Validate over the null.}

\appendix
\section{Platform Validation and Infrastructure}
\label{app:platform}

Zetyra spans four design domains: frequentist methods (group sequential design,
survival sample size, CUPED); Bayesian methods
\citep{zetyra_bayesian_toolkit} (prior elicitation, historical
borrowing via MAP and power priors, sequential monitoring, predictive power);
adaptive methods (blinded, unblinded, and single-arm sample size re-estimation
(SSR); response-adaptive randomization (RAR) via Thompson Sampling and doubly
adaptive biased coin designs); and master protocol settings (basket, umbrella,
platform trials).

\paragraph{Reference engines.}
Frequentist benchmarking uses gsDesign \citep{anderson_gsdesign} for group
sequential boundaries and \citet{schoenfeld_1983} for survival sample size.
SSR rules are checked against conditional-power formulas by self-consistency
testing rather than against an external reference design.  Bayesian
benchmarking uses RBesT \citep{weber_rbest} for MAP prior construction and
\citet{zhou_ji_2024} for sequential monitoring boundaries.  Predictive-probability
monitoring is benchmarked against \citet{lee_liu_2008} and
\citet{saville_2014}.  RAR designs are benchmarked against analytical
$P(\theta_e > \theta_c)$ computed via numerical integration.

\paragraph{Clinical trial benchmarks.}
Eight published trials plus one methodological worked example provide
outcome-level comparison.  The eight trials are the Salk polio vaccine field
trial; DAPA-HF; PACIFIC (OS); MONALEESA-7 (OS); I-SPY~2
\citep{barker_ispy2}; STAMPEDE \citep{sydes_stampede_2012}; REMAP-CAP
\citep{angus_remapcap}; and NCT03377023 \citep{nct03377023}.  Primary
references for the Salk, DAPA-HF, PACIFIC and MONALEESA-7 benchmarks are
recorded in the validation repository rather than reproduced here.  The
methodological worked example is \citet{leyrat_2024}, a cluster-randomized
trial sample-size calculation involving the intracluster correlation and design
effect---not a trial outcome benchmark.  Maximum deviation across the eight
trials: 0.034 z-score units.

\paragraph{Automated testing.}
The repository documents 896 validation checks across 42 scripts (39 Python, 3
R) and reports all as passing.  Continuous integration runs a six-script smoke
subset on every push and pull request; the full suite is executed locally
rather than in CI.  The repository is open to independent verification:
\href{https://github.com/evidenceinthewild/zetyra-validation}{github.com/evidenceinthewild/zetyra-validation}.
Full documentation is available in the Zetyra white paper, v2.3
\citep{zetyra_whitepaper}; the Bayesian calculators used here are documented
separately in the Zetyra Bayesian Toolkit \citep{zetyra_bayesian_toolkit}.

\paragraph{Scope of these checks.}
These are numerical benchmark checks---each verifies that an implementation
reproduces a reference computation or is internally self-consistent.  None is
an operating-characteristic evaluation, and passing them does not establish
Type~I error control for any design in which the components are combined.  That
distinction is the subject of this paper.
